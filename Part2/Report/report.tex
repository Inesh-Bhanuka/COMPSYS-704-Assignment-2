\title{Real-Time Step Counting and Heading Estimation on the SensorTile}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Group 3: \tbd{member names and UPIs}}
\IEEEauthorblockA{CS704 Project 2, University of Auckland}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We present a body-worn tracker for factory workers built on the STMicroelectronics
SensorTile. The firmware counts steps, estimates walked distance and reports heading in real
time, and streams the results to a phone over Bluetooth Low Energy. Steps are found by
adaptive peak and valley detection on the acceleration magnitude, gated by signal variance.
Heading comes from a tilt-compensated, self-calibrating magnetometer blended with the
on-board gyroscope, so turns are tracked through magnetic disturbances that are common near
steel. The direction of travel is either learnt from the accelerometer while walking or set
with a hand gesture, so the board can be worn in any orientation, including a trouser
pocket. On a 44-step route walked with the board in a pocket, every step was counted. On a
repeat of the route, the two long legs were within 3 degrees of a phone compass and the mean
heading error over all legs was 7 degrees.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
pedestrian dead reckoning, step detection, electronic compass, sensor fusion, MEMS
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
AICorp wants to monitor employee movement inside a factory, where GPS is unavailable and
installing infrastructure is costly. Step-based pedestrian dead reckoning (PDR) needs only a
body-worn inertial unit: each detected step advances the position by a step length $L_k$ in
heading $\psi_k$~\cite{jin2011,tian2016}. The device must therefore produce a step count and
an orientation, reliably and in real time.

Our literature review found that adaptive thresholds on the orientation-invariant
magnitude $|\mathbf{a}|$ work without knowing how the device is carried~\cite{lee2015}, and
that a variance-based walk gate suppresses false steps~\cite{brajdic2013}. A magnetometer
heading must be tilt-compensated and protected from magnetic disturbance, usually by fusing
a gyroscope~\cite{jin2011,yuan2015,kuang2018}, and the offset between the device and the
walking direction must be estimated~\cite{tian2016}.

The brief specifies an accelerometer and magnetometer. In our first tests, in a laboratory
lined with steel benches, a compass-only heading froze for minutes at a time, so we also
used the LSM6DSM gyroscope already fitted to the SensorTile. Our contributions are a step
detector that needs no knowledge of placement; a gyro-aided, tilt-compensated compass with
automatic calibration and disturbance rejection; two ways of finding the direction of travel
in the board's axes, one learnt from the gait and one set by a gesture; and a
record-and-replay workflow that runs logged sensor data through the unmodified firmware
algorithms on a PC, which produced every result in Section~\ref{sec:perf}.

\section{System Overview}
\subsection{Hardware and Sensor Configuration}
The SensorTile (STM32L476JG, Cortex-M4F at 80\,MHz) is programmed through an STLINK-V3. Both
sensors share one 3-wire SPI bus with their I\textsuperscript{2}C interfaces disabled. The
LSM303AGR accelerometer runs in 12-bit high-resolution mode at 50\,Hz with a $\pm4$\,g range,
which leaves headroom for heel strikes at 1.95\,mg/LSB. Its magnetometer runs continuously at
100\,Hz with temperature compensation, at 1.5\,mG/LSB~\cite{an4825}. The LSM6DSM gyroscope
runs at 52\,Hz with a $\pm500$\,dps range (17.5\,mdps/LSB); its accelerometer is left off.
Block data update is enabled on all three, so a read never mixes two samples.

The sensors do not share axes. A clockwise turn on a desk decreased our first heading, which
showed that the LSM303AGR's axes form a left-handed set on this board (ST's own SensorTile
configuration lists the magnetometer as south-west-up). We negate the $y$ axis of both its
sensors. The LSM6DSM is rotated by $90^\circ$ and maps as $(x,y,z)=(g_y,-g_x,g_z)$, found by
ranking all 48 axis permutations by how well the gyro explains the rotation of the measured
field, $\dot{\mathbf{m}}=-\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{m}$. Such checks cannot detect a
mirror image, so the desk test is needed as well.

\subsection{Software Structure}
Timer TIM4 raises a flag every 20\,ms. The main loop then reads the three sensors, converts
them to mg, mG and mdps in a common frame, and calls two independent modules
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}): \texttt{StepDetector\_Update()} and \texttt{Heading\_Update()}. Each
keeps its state in one private structure, has its tuning constants in its header, and
exposes a debug structure. Results go out in the existing BLE motion characteristic: the
accelerometer and magnetometer slots carry raw data, and the gyroscope slots carry steps,
heading and distance, so the provided ST BLE Sensor app plots all three. Heading events
(calibrated, forward set, forward locked) light the LED for 0.5\,s, and forward set also
zeroes the step count.

A compile-time switch prints every raw sample over UART at 115200 baud. A host program,
\texttt{replay.c}, feeds such a log through the same \texttt{step\_detector.c} and
\texttt{heading.c} that run on the board, and a Python script plots every internal
variable, so a tuning change can be tested on the same walk without reflashing or walking
again.

\section{Step Detection}
A sensor on the trunk or thigh sees one rise of $|\mathbf{a}|$ above 1\,g at heel strike and
push-off, then one dip below 1\,g as the body falls into the next step. The detector counts
one step per peak--valley cycle, with $O(1)$ work per sample.

\subsubsection{Pre-processing}
We use the magnitude $|\mathbf{a}|=\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2}$, which does not depend on how
the board is mounted~\cite{lee2015}. A 5-tap moving average (100\,ms) removes jitter above
about 4\,Hz to give $f_k$. The first 0.5\,s after reset is discarded while the sensor
settles.

\subsubsection{Adaptive thresholds}
A running mean and variance are tracked with exponential moving averages over $N=50$
samples ($\approx$1\,s):
\begin{equation}
\mu_k=\mu_{k-1}+\tfrac{f_k-\mu_{k-1}}{N},\quad
\sigma_k^2=\sigma_{k-1}^2+\tfrac{(f_k-\mu_{k-1})^2-\sigma_{k-1}^2}{N}.
\end{equation}
The thresholds are $T_\pm=\mu_k\pm\max(0.3\,\sigma_k,\,35\,\text{mg})$. They follow the
walker's intensity, as in~\cite{lee2015}, while the floor keeps them out of the sensor noise.

\subsubsection{Peak--valley state machine}
In the \emph{seek-peak} state, the largest $f_k$ above $T_+$ is recorded, and the peak ends
when $f_k$ falls back below $\mu_k$. In the \emph{seek-valley} state, the smallest $f_k$
below $T_-$ is recorded, and the cycle completes when $f_k$ rises back above $\mu_k$. If no
valley follows within 1\,s, the peak is discarded.

\subsubsection{Validation}
A completed cycle counts as a step only if all of the following hold:
(i) the variance gate is open ($\sigma_k>40$\,mg), so standing still produces no steps,
following Brajdic and Harle's walk detector~\cite{brajdic2013};
(ii) the peak-to-valley swing is at least 120\,mg;
(iii) the time since the previous step is at least
$\max(250\,\text{ms},\,0.5\,\bar T)$, where $\bar T$ is an adaptive average of recent step
intervals ($\bar T\leftarrow\bar T+0.2(\Delta t-\bar T)$). This rejects double peaks within
one step.
A walk starts only after two consecutive valid steps, which are then credited together, so
an isolated knock or sitting down is never counted. A gap of more than 2\,s ends the walk.
Every rejected cycle is labelled with its reason (\emph{still}, \emph{small}, \emph{fast} or
\emph{timeout}), which showed which constant to change while tuning.

\subsubsection{Step length}
Each step's length uses the Weinberg model~\cite{weinberg2002},
$L=K\sqrt[4]{a_{\max}-a_{\min}}$, with the swing in m/s\textsuperscript{2} and $K=0.45$.

\section{Heading Estimation}
The heading is the direction of travel in degrees clockwise from magnetic north. Board tilt,
the board's own magnetic distortion, external disturbances, gyroscope drift, and the unknown
orientation of the board on the body all have to be handled.

\subsubsection{Gravity estimate}
With $\boldsymbol{\omega}_k$ the bias-corrected gyro rate, the gravity estimate is rotated
with the board and then pulled towards the accelerometer:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_k=\mathbf{g}_{k-1}-(\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\times\mathbf{g}_{k-1})\,\Delta t,\qquad
\mathbf{g}_k\leftarrow\mathbf{g}_k+\alpha_g(\mathbf{a}_k-\mathbf{g}_k),
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_g=0.02$ ($\approx$1\,s), and $\mathbf{D}=-\mathbf{g}/|\mathbf{g}|$. The low-pass
keeps walking accelerations from tilting the frame. Without the gyro step, the estimate lagged
behind a pocketed board swinging $\pm30^\circ$ with the thigh, and a replayed pocket walk
measured a $180^\circ$ turn as $159^\circ$; with it, the same walk gave $175^\circ$.

\subsubsection{Hard/soft-iron calibration}
The battery and PCB add a constant offset (hard iron, 350\,mG on $z$ on our board) and scale
each axis differently (soft iron). After power-up the wearer points each face of the board
up in turn while the firmware tracks each axis's extremes. The offset is
$o_i=(\max_i+\min_i)/2$, the radius $r_i=(\max_i-\min_i)/2$, $R=\tfrac13\sum r_i$, and the
corrected field $m_i=(m^{\text{raw}}_i-o_i)\,R/r_i$. Calibration takes effect once every axis
has swung by at least 800\,mG (Auckland's 550\,mG field gives about 1100\,mG). Because one
stray magnet stretches the bounds, the calibration is frozen when the forward direction is
set, and holding the board face down for 3\,s restarts it.

\subsubsection{Tilt-compensated magnetic heading}
Rather than computing roll and pitch angles, we build the North--East--Down frame directly
in sensor coordinates:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{D}\times\mathbf{m},\qquad \mathbf{N}=\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{D},\qquad
\psi_m=\operatorname{atan2}(\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{E},\;\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{N}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{f}$ is the horizontal unit vector, in board axes, that points in the walking
direction. The Earth's field lies in the North--Down plane, so $\mathbf{D}\times\mathbf{m}$
always points East. This is valid at any tilt, has no Euler-angle singularities, and costs
one square root and one $\operatorname{atan2}$ per sample.

\subsubsection{Gyro integration and bias}
A rotation about $\mathbf{D}$ is a clockwise turn seen from above, so the gyro advances the
heading by $(\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\cdot\mathbf{D}_k)\,\Delta t$ at any tilt. Our LSM6DSM's
zero-rate offset of about 3\,dps would drift the heading by $180^\circ$ per minute, so it is
learnt while the board is still: when the accelerometer and gyro are within 30\,mg and
2\,dps of their recent averages and the gyro reads below 15\,dps, the bias is low-pass
filtered towards the raw reading. The 15\,dps cap stops a slow turn being learnt as bias.

\subsubsection{Fusion and disturbance rejection}
The heading is a complementary filter:
\begin{equation}
\psi_k=\psi_{k-1}+(\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\cdot\mathbf{D}_k)\Delta t
      +\tfrac{\Delta t}{\tau}\,\mathrm{wrap}\!\left(\psi_{m,k}-\psi_{k-1}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{wrap}$ maps an angle into $(-180^\circ,180^\circ]$ and $\tau=10$\,s. The gyro
follows turns immediately, and the magnetometer removes its drift. On replayed lab walks,
$\tau=10$\,s halved the step-to-step wobble compared with $\tau=2$\,s. A clean field has
magnitude $\approx R$; if $\big||\mathbf{m}|-R\big|>0.2R$, for example next to steel benches
or motors, the magnetic term is dropped and the gyro carries the heading alone, as with the
quasi-static field detection of~\cite{kuang2018}. The heading is set to $\psi_m$ directly
when calibration completes and when the forward direction is set.

\subsubsection{Direction of travel}
$\mathbf{f}$ is chosen in one of three ways. By default it is the board's $+X$ axis. While
the wearer walks, it is learnt from the gait: the trunk rocks forwards and backwards with
each step more than it sways sideways, so the main axis of the horizontal acceleration is
the walking line. The firmware removes gravity and a 0.2\,s mean from the acceleration,
splits the result into a vertical part $u$ and a horizontal part $\mathbf{h}$, and tracks
$\mathbf{C}\leftarrow\mathbf{C}+\alpha_w(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{h}^{\mathsf T}-\mathbf{C})$ with
$\alpha_w=0.01$. One power-iteration step per sample,
$\mathbf{v}\leftarrow\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}/|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}|$, follows its principal axis.
Which end of $\mathbf{v}$ is forward comes from the timing of the swing against the bounce:
with $s=\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{v}$, our waist and hand recordings showed that the low-passed
product $u(t-80\,\text{ms})\,\big(s(t)-s(t-240\,\text{ms})\big)$ is positive when
$\mathbf{v}$ points forward. This resembles the offset compensation of~\cite{tian2016}; unlike
the PCA that Jin et~al.\ found unreliable below 25\,Hz~\cite{jin2011}, it runs at 50\,Hz with
a gyro-stabilised vertical. Learning runs only while the horizontal RMS is 40--350\,mg and the
gyro RMS is below 80\,dps (walking measured 11--74\,dps, handling 110--150\,dps), and its
result replaces the default after 3\,s of continuous walking.

A trouser pocket follows the thigh, not the trunk, so there the wearer instead holds the
board flat, face up, with $+X$ ahead for 2\,s. The firmware captures $\mathbf{f}=+X$ and
rotates it against the board's own rotation,
$\mathbf{f}\leftarrow\mathbf{f}-(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{f})\Delta t$, while the board
is put away, so that it stays fixed in the world. It locks $\mathbf{f}$ to the board once the
board has moved and settled (gyro RMS above 80\,dps, then below 30\,dps, or after 8\,s).
Whenever $\mathbf{f}$ changes within the board, the heading is rotated by
$\operatorname{atan2}((\mathbf{f}_{\text{old}}\times\mathbf{f})\cdot\mathbf{D},\,
\mathbf{f}_{\text{old}}\cdot\mathbf{f})$, so it never jumps.

\section{Performance Analysis}\label{sec:perf}
\subsection{Validation in Simulation}
A host-side test (\texttt{test/test\_heading.c}) runs \texttt{heading.c} on synthetic data
in a field approximating Auckland's (250\,mG north, 490\,mG up), distorted by a hard-iron
offset of $(120,-80,200)$\,mG and soft-iron scales of $(1.15,0.9,1.0)$, after a simulated
tumble. Every case in Table~\ref{tab:sim} passes. This confirms the geometry, calibration,
gyro path and gestures, but not noise, sway or real field distortion.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
All trials used one subject in the university's engineering building. Each was recorded over
UART with the board tethered to a laptop and then replayed through the final firmware code;
logs taken before the axis correction of Section~II-A were converted to the corrected frame
first. Before the heading trials the wearer did the routine of Section~IV: face down,
tumble, set forward, put the board away. The reference direction came from a phone compass
set to magnetic north, held away from the board. In separate runs the board started flat
with a known axis forward and was then placed at the waist or held in the hand; integrating
the gyro over those few seconds gave a compass-free reference for the forward axis. Most
tests were done on level~7, where steel benches distort the field: its strength varied from
413 to 894\,mG along one walk.

\subsection{Step Counting}
Table~\ref{tab:steps} lists the step counts. Walking with the board in a trouser pocket,
the detector was exact or within 4\%, with no change needed for the placement because
$|\mathbf{a}|$ ignores orientation. No step was counted in any stationary period we
recorded, including standing at turn-arounds. In one 4-minute session the detector rejected
27 cycles: 14 \emph{timeout}, 8 \emph{small}, 3 \emph{still} and 2 \emph{fast}. The main
weakness is handling: tumbling the board for calibration produced 29 false steps, and the
full routine before a walk about 14, because shaking the board looks like walking to an
accelerometer-only detector. The final firmware therefore zeroes the count at the
forward-set gesture, which ends the routine. The median swing of counted steps was 381\,mg, for which the
Weinberg model gives 0.63\,m, a plausible adult step. We did not walk a measured course, so
distance has not been validated and $K$ was not calibrated for the wearer.

\subsection{Heading}
Table~\ref{tab:heading} gives a second run of the reference route: steps ahead, a right
turn, a turn-around and a left turn, with the board in a pocket. The wearer pointed the board's $-X$

The gyro fusion mattered most near steel. Before it, the heading froze for about 2.5\,min
while the board sat in a disturbed spot. With it, the compass was rejected along the far end
of the reference route (field 1.6--1.9 times normal) and the gyro carried the heading. On a
route on a lower floor the compass was rejected for 69\% of the walk, and the gyro alone
measured a $90^\circ$ turn as $83^\circ$ and the turn-arounds as 170--175$^\circ$.

Against the gyro-integrated reference, the gesture-carried forward was within $5^\circ$ at
the waist and $9^\circ$ in the hand, and the gait-learnt forward within 3--11$^\circ$ and
9--16$^\circ$; the two methods agreed to within $4.2^\circ$ and $2.3^\circ$. In a pocket the
gait method did not converge, as expected, and the default axis was kept.

Calibration quality was the largest practical source of error. Our first tumble gave
$R=551$\,mG against Auckland's $\approx$550\,mG. Two later tumbles, done while holding the
tethered laptop, stretched one axis to 1576\,mG, beyond the $\approx$1100\,mG the Earth's
field can produce, and gave $R=616$--$628$\,mG. With $R$ 12\% too large, good samples fail
the 20\% gate more often, which probably contributed to the 69\% rejection rate above.

\subsection{Computational Cost}
Both modules do fixed work per sample. Built with GCC 14.2 (\texttt{-Og}), the step detector takes
1.3\,kB of code and 128\,B of RAM, and the heading module 4.4\,kB and 328\,B. The complete
firmware is 35.8\,kB of code and 15.1\,kB of RAM, 3.5\% of the flash and 12\% of the RAM of
the STM32L476JG. Even with a UART line printed every sample ($\approx$4\,ms), the loop kept
its 50\,Hz rate.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Handling counts as steps.} Zeroing the count at the gesture drops the
calibration tumble, but putting the board away can still add a few. The gyro RMS separates
handling (110--150\,dps) from walking (11--74\,dps) and could gate the step detector.
\item \emph{Calibration} is lost at every reset and is stretched by any magnet near the
board during the tumble; freezing it only protects it afterwards. An ellipsoid fit stored in
flash would be more robust.
\item \emph{Disturbance gate.} Only $|\mathbf{m}|$ is checked. At one spot the magnitude was
within 18\% of normal but the dip angle was $-19^\circ$, against $-54^\circ$ elsewhere in the
room, so a distorted heading passed. A dip-angle check would reject it. In a long
disturbance the gyro-only heading drifts slowly.
\item \emph{Placement.} The gait-learnt direction works at the waist and in the hand but not
in a pocket, where the gesture is needed, the heading sways $\pm10$--$15^\circ$ per stride,
and the gesture must be repeated if the board shifts.
\item \emph{Reference and scope.} Headings are magnetic; true north needs a declination of
about $+20^\circ$ in Auckland. $K$ is per user and the Weinberg model ignores step
frequency~\cite{tian2016}. One subject walked a few routes against a phone compass; stairs,
running and other walkers were not tested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
The firmware meets the brief: it counts steps and reports orientation in real time over
BLE, in two small modules that can be tested on a PC. In pocket trials the step count was
exact or within 4\%, and the heading was within $3^\circ$ of a phone compass on long legs,
with a mean error of $7.4^\circ$ over a route with three turns. Blending the gyroscope
turned heading loss near steel into a slow drift, and learning or carrying the forward
direction removed the need to mount the board one fixed way.
